## SpaceX Just Got Permission to Put a Million AI Data Centers in Space

The FCC has authorized SpaceX to deploy up to 1 million AI chip-equipped satellites as part of its "Starmind" initiative — the first full constellation approval of its kind. Google and Blue Origin are also chasing the space-based AI compute dream, but SpaceX just lapped the field on the regulatory front. This is orbital infrastructure at a scale that would have sounded like science fiction five years ago.

**Why it matters:** Putting AI compute in orbit redefines where and how the world's most critical AI workloads can run — with zero land, zero permits, and theoretically global coverage.

**Source:** [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/science/1042278/spacex-fcc-starmind-ai-space-data-centers)

## Google's AI TV Patent Is the Dystopian Streaming Future Nobody Asked For

Google filed a patent for "My Channel," a system that generates personalized AI video content and ads based on everything Google knows about you. The patent was rejected — for now — but Google can appeal or refile, meaning this thing could still find its way to your living room. Imagine YouTube, but every video is algorithmically manufactured specifically to hold your attention and sell you stuff.

**Why it matters:** This is a preview of where ad-supported streaming goes when AI gets cheap enough — and it should scare every human creator paying attention.

**Source:** [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/entertainment/1042221/google-my-channel-ai-video-patent)

## Google's Familiar Faces AI Comes to Walmart's $35 Security Camera

Google Home's Familiar Faces feature — which uses AI to recognize people around your home — is now supported on Walmart's budget Onn cameras, starting at just $35. You'll still need a Google Home Premium subscription to unlock it, so the camera is cheap but the AI isn't free. This is the clearest sign yet that on-device AI recognition is trickling down from premium hardware into mass-market gear.

**Why it matters:** When face recognition AI ships on a $35 camera at Walmart, it stops being a niche feature and becomes ambient infrastructure in millions of homes.

**Source:** [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/tech/1042938/google-home-familiar-faces-onn-cameras)

## 1Password Got Breached — Your Passwords Are Safe, But Your Inbox Isn't

A hacker accessed 1Password's backend systems and walked away with customer names and email addresses. Vault contents and actual passwords were not exposed, but the stolen data is a phishing goldmine — attackers now have a list of people who use a password manager and can craft very convincing follow-up attacks. The company started investigating on November 19th and the number of affected users hasn't been disclosed.

**Why it matters:** A breach at a password manager — even one that doesn't expose passwords — is a high-value target for social engineering, and it erodes trust in the exact services people rely on for security.

**Source:** [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/tech/1042409/1password-data-breach-leak)

## Backblaze Is Sharing Your Data With Advertisers — Here's Their Explanation

Backblaze confirmed it's sharing customer information with advertisers, though it insists your actual backup and storage files aren't part of the deal. The company offered an explanation to Ars Technica, but the fact that a cloud backup service — where people store some of their most sensitive files — is in the ad data business at all is raising eyebrows. The line between "account metadata" and "personal data" gets blurrier every year.

**Why it matters:** If your backup provider is monetizing your information, the implicit trust contract between user and cloud storage service is broken, full stop.

**Source:** [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/information-technology/2026/11/backblaze-explains-why-its-sharing-customer-information-with-advertisers/)

## Amazon Can't Escape Responsibility for Dangerous Third-Party Products

A federal court ruled that Amazon is a distributor — not just a logistics provider — making it legally responsible for recalling over 400,000 hazardous third-party products. Amazon had sued the CPSC to dodge the obligation, and lost. This ruling has teeth: it forces the world's largest e-commerce platform to take ownership of what flows through its marketplace.

**Why it matters:** This sets a legal precedent that platform scale doesn't shield you from product liability — and it could reshape how Amazon (and others) vet third-party sellers going forward.

**Source:** [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/tech/1042870/amazon-recall-order-cpsc)
